## Graphical abstract summary (≤100 words)

Stool microbiome tests for colorectal cancer are judged by AUROC, but in use they work at a single cut-off. A cut-off set for 90% specificity on published cohorts gave false-positive rates of 0–37% at 11 colorectal cancer cohorts, far more variable than chance. Severe disease (cirrhosis, atherosclerosis) raised scores through oral-origin marker taxa, and in a diabetes cohort metformin use tracked the excess (exploratory). Resetting the cut-off locally from unlabelled scores restored specificity; a cut-off updated from colonoscopy results of test-positive people also recovered from drift. Both are in the cutoffguard tool.

To the Editor,

Stool metagenomic tests detect colorectal cancer (CRC) with an area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of about 0.8 across countries [1, 2]. In use, however, a test reports positive or negative at one cut-off, usually chosen on published case-control cohorts to give a target specificity such as 90%. FIT-based screening programmes choose and adjust their positivity cut-offs for their own population [6]; microbiome tests have no such practice. Two kinds of shift can break the specificity of a fixed cut-off. First, sites differ in sequencing, diet and population, the familiar batch effect [3]. Second, the people tested include patients with other diseases or drugs that change the gut microbiome; CRC signatures are known to fire in samples from other diseases [2, 4], and many microbiome changes are shared across diseases [5]. Studies rarely report the specificity that a fixed cut-off actually achieves at a new site or in specific patient groups, or how to restore it without a new labelled study.

Here, we examined this in 32 public cohorts from curatedMetagenomicData [7] (Table S1). We fixed methods and endpoints in a protocol before analysing the held-out cohorts, and analysed them once; an earlier pilot had already measured the false-positive rate at all 11 CRC cohorts, so that part of the audit was not blinded. A medication analysis was fixed in a second protocol before it was run. Analyses added later are marked as post hoc. In short, a cut-off set for 90% specificity on published cohorts exceeded its target at about half of the CRC cohorts, and resetting it locally restored the intended specificity.

## CUT-OFFS SET ON PUBLISHED COHORTS DO NOT HOLD AT NEW CRC SITES

We trained L1-penalised logistic regression (LASSO) and random forest classifiers with fixed settings and set the cut-off at 90% specificity on out-of-fold source controls (Methods S2–S3). In a post hoc audit, we evaluated this cut-off at all 11 CRC cohorts: three locked external sites (models trained on eight development cohorts) and the eight development cohorts by leave-one-cohort-out. Because the audit evaluates a fixed cut-off and involves no method choice, development cohorts can serve as audit sites. The realised false-positive rate (FPR) ranged from 0.00 to 0.37 (Figure 1B). It exceeded 0.12 at 6 of 11 cohorts for each classifier, and the exact 95% confidence interval lay entirely above the 10% target at five (LASSO) and three (random forest) cohorts. The differences between cohorts were far larger than chance alone would produce with these numbers of controls (p < 10^−15^; Table S12). Because sensitivity rose and fell with the FPR, the AUROC alone would not reveal this. Classifiers for IBD and type 2 diabetes (T2D) showed the same failure (FPR 0.00–0.75; Table S12). Site-wise standardisation of features, a simple label-free batch correction, reduced the spread but did not remove it (post hoc, development cohorts above 0.12: LASSO 50% to 25%, random forest 38% to 25%; Table S7).

Why do some cohorts fail? For each CRC cohort we split the shift of its control scores into species contributions (post hoc; Table S14). The FPR followed the total shift (Spearman ρ = 0.88), but its make-up differed between cohorts: depleted commensals in the Indian cohort, other enriched taxa in a French and an American cohort, and oral-origin taxa together with depletion in the two Japanese cohorts. No single component and no recorded property of the controls (sequencing depth, diversity, age) predicted the FPR (all p > 0.25). Site failures are therefore not foreseeable from simple metadata, and must be measured locally.

## OVER-FLAGGED PATIENT GROUPS DIFFER BETWEEN SETTINGS

We compared the CRC score of people with other conditions with that of controls from the same cohort (Figure 1C). In development cohorts, the score was much higher in liver cirrhosis [8] (+1.30 logit after adjustment for age, BMI and sex; 95% CI 0.99–1.60) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [9] (+0.87, 0.62–1.12). Effects were smaller for ME/CFS (+0.39, 0.12–0.67) and hypertension (+0.33, unadjusted), and absent for fatty liver, asthma and schizophrenia. In a post hoc check inside three CRC cohorts, colonoscopy-negative controls with recorded hypertension, fatty liver, T2D or hypercholesterolaemia were not flagged more often than healthy controls (4% vs 16%; adjusted score difference −0.19, −0.60 to 0.21). Mild cardiometabolic labels were therefore not associated with more false positives in these cohorts.

In the locked MetaCardis cohort [10], our pre-specified test, adjusted for sex, found higher scores in heart failure (+0.33, 0.14–0.51) and T2D (+0.35, 0.25–0.45; LASSO, both Holm-adjusted p < 0.01; random forest p = 0.017 and < 10^−4^). Coronary artery disease and prediabetes showed no increase; in a second T2D cohort the increase was significant only for random forest, and a third showed none (Table S3). Because these groups differ in country and medication, we then added both to the model (post hoc). The heart-failure effect was attenuated: it remained significant for LASSO (+0.28, 0.06–0.50) but not for random forest (+0.13, p = 0.11), nor in a complete-case analysis that also adjusted for age and BMI (n = 1,310; Table S9). The T2D effect largely disappeared (+0.11, −0.02 to 0.24), and prediabetes became nominally positive (LASSO +0.12, p = 0.044). Metformin strongly reshapes the gut microbiome [11]. Within T2D, metformin users were test-positive 2.4 times as often as non-users (20.5% vs 8.5%; adjusted +0.44, p < 10^−6^), and non-users were flagged at a rate similar to controls (10.3%). In a smaller cohort the difference had the same sign but was not significant (LASSO +0.40, p = 0.18), and two cohorts with 15 and 20 users showed none (Table S6d). Because metformin use also marks treated, longer-standing diabetes, confounding by indication cannot be excluded; the finding is exploratory.

Decomposing each group's score gap into species contributions (exploratory; Methods S5) showed two routes (Figure 1D). In cirrhosis and atherosclerotic disease, 78–83% of the gap came from oral-origin taxa that are also hallmark CRC markers, such as *Streptococcus oralis*, *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, *Veillonella parvula* and *Gemella morbillorum* [1, 2, 12]. Removing such shared species from the signature cost cancer detection (AUROC 0.76 to 0.72) before it removed the excess (Table S4). In locked heart failure and T2D, and for metformin, oral taxa contributed little (≤0.05 logit); depleted commensal fermenters and other enriched taxa dominated (Table S9b). Our pre-specified medication analysis supports the oral route: proton-pump inhibitors, which carry oral taxa into the gut [13], raised the oral part of the score (+0.18, 0.14–0.21). Their effect on the total LASSO score was not significant (+0.04, p = 0.24), so that primary hypothesis was not supported; the secondary random-forest model showed a small increase (+0.09, p = 0.014). Which groups are over-flagged therefore differs between settings and cannot be fully predicted in advance.

## LOCAL RECALIBRATION RESTORES SPECIFICITY; COLONOSCOPY FEEDBACK HELPS UNDER DRIFT

We simulated 12-month deployments in which each cohort played the role of a screening site with known true FPR and sensitivity (Methods S4), and compared four cut-offs: the fixed cut-off from published cohorts; a local quantile of the site's unlabelled scores, which assumes nobody tested has cancer; a prevalence-bounded local cut-off, which corrects that quantile for an assumed maximum share of cancers among those tested [14]; and a colonoscopy-feedback cut-off. In screening, people who test positive have a colonoscopy, so their true status becomes known [15]. Each month, the colonoscopy-feedback rule looks at the people above a candidate cut-off, counts those with a negative colonoscopy, and, conservatively, everyone whose result is not yet known, as false positives. It chooses the lowest cut-off at which this pessimistic FPR is still 10% or less. It starts with one deliberately liberal month to collect colonoscopy results, and restarts its count when a month has more false positives than chance allows. Provided the site's population does not change within the counting window, cancer prevalence stays below the assumed maximum, and colonoscopy does not mislabel controls as cancers, the average FPR stays at or below 10% however referral, attendance or reporting delays behave (Proposition S1). Related statistical methods require labelled local controls or a model of the shift [16–18]; neither local rule does.

At the 11 CRC cohorts (1% prevalence; post hoc), the fixed cut-off kept a mean FPR of 0.14, with 55% of site-months above 0.12 (Figure 2A). All local cut-offs removed most of this error: local quantile 0.11 (21% of months above 0.12), prevalence-bounded 0.10 (13%) and colonoscopy-feedback 0.083 (0.3%), with sensitivity 0.50 against 0.52 for an oracle cut-off set on each site's true controls. Eight of these cohorts are development cohorts on which the feedback rule was designed; at the three locked CRC sites alone, the values were 0.19 (83% of months above 0.12), 0.099 (7%) and 0.087 (0.4%) for the fixed, prevalence-bounded and feedback cut-offs. In the pre-specified analysis across all 16 held-out sites, the feedback cut-off beat the fixed cut-off (difference in months above 0.12: −0.46, 95% CI −0.66 to −0.20; p = 0.011; significant for CRC but not separately for IBD, T2D or adenoma) and the local quantile (p = 2.5 × 10^−7^), with sensitivity within 0.015 of the oracle. Its small advantage over the prevalence-bounded cut-off (p = 0.049) came from cohorts with few controls. At the nine cohorts with at least 40 controls (post hoc), the two did not differ detectably (mean FPR 0.090 vs 0.083; p = 0.72), and neither was significantly better than the fixed cut-off (p = 0.074), which at some large cohorts was too strict rather than too lenient. With smoothed site populations, the prevalence-bounded cut-off exceeded 0.12 less often (0.4% vs 2.6% of months; Table S2).

We then removed the resampling (post hoc; Figure 2D). At the three held-out cohorts with at least 100 controls (four cohort–classifier pairs), each sample entered once in random order, and the FPR was measured on controls not yet seen; at MetaCardis, controls also entered country by country, a real shift between sites. All local cut-offs kept the mean FPR near target, but single months often exceeded 0.12 (prevalence-bounded 15%, feedback 18% in random order). Stricter versions of both rules, added after the locked analysis to keep most single months on target, reduced this to 3–5% in random order and to 12–17% under the country shift, where the standard local rules exceeded 0.12 in 27% of months and the fixed cut-off, too strict at this cohort, in 1%. At stable, well-sampled sites, therefore, the strict prevalence-bounded cut-off, which needs no colonoscopy results, is sufficient.

The colonoscopy-feedback cut-off helped mainly where label-free rules cannot. After an upward drift of all scores by 0.5 logit, for example from a new kit lot (specified in the frozen analysis code), its restart restored the FPR within one to two months, while the label-free rules, which have no restart, stayed high (months 10–12 at 1% prevalence: 0.082 vs 0.174 and 0.183; Figure 2B). At 10% prevalence, the prevalence-bounded cut-off became conservative (locked CRC sites: FPR 0.057 vs 0.090; sensitivity 0.44 vs 0.52). At MetaCardis, where the CRC test was applied to a cardiometabolic cohort, recalibrating everyone together kept the overall FPR at 0.095, but heart failure stayed at 0.21 and T2D at 0.14. Recalibrating within recorded groups brought every group to 0.08–0.09 (Figure 2C; heart failure still exceeded 0.12 in 15% of months), and the label-free rule did so as well as the feedback rule (post hoc; Table S11). Because comorbid CRC patients did not score higher (Table S8), this raises the cut-off and lowers sensitivity in over-flagged groups. At the locked CRC sites, the feedback cut-off avoided 83 false-positive referrals per 1,000 people screened but detected 1.1 fewer cancers per 1,000 than the too-lenient fixed cut-off (sensitivity 0.52 vs 0.64). In net-benefit terms, local recalibration is preferable unless a programme would accept more than 75 false-positive colonoscopies to detect one additional cancer (post hoc; Table S15). Its first, liberal month refers about a third of people at the CRC sites (33%). After a downward drift, all rules stayed too strict for the rest of the simulated period (post hoc; Table S2c).

## CONCLUSION

The specificity of a microbiome CRC test does not travel. It changes with the site, for reasons that differ between cohorts and are not visible in simple metadata, and in some settings with the patients tested, through severe disease and oral-origin taxa or possibly metformin (one cohort). Local recalibration is therefore needed. At stable, well-sampled sites a prevalence-bounded local cut-off in its strict form (added post hoc) suffices, and it needs no labels. A cut-off updated from colonoscopy results of test-positive people adds recovery from large upward drift and stays efficient at higher prevalence. Both, with a site audit, are released as the open-source package cutoffguard. Our deployments were simulated from case-control cohorts with small control sets; the pilot had seen all CRC cohorts; colonoscopy was treated as a perfect reference; all rules stayed too strict after downward drift; and the medication findings rest mainly on one cohort. We recommend that microbiome test studies report the realised specificity at each external site and in major patient groups, check common medications, and plan local recalibration before deployment.
