We submit our manuscript, **"Gut microbiome cancer tests mistake chronic disease for cancer, and routine colonoscopy outcomes can reset their cut-offs"**, for consideration as a Letter (Short Communication) in *iMeta*.

Stool microbiome classifiers for colorectal cancer are moving towards use in screening, and *iMeta* has published several cross-cohort microbiome signatures. These classifiers are almost always validated by AUROC, but a test in a programme works at one fixed cut-off. Our Letter shows three things that matter for real deployment:

1. **Cut-offs do not transfer.** Cut-offs set for 90% specificity on published cohorts gave false-positive rates from 0% to 75% across 16 locked test sites in four diseases. AUROC-based validation does not reveal this.
2. **The cancer test reacts to chronic disease.** Within the same cohort, people with heart failure and type 2 diabetes were flagged more often than healthy people (confirmed once in locked cohorts under a pre-registered protocol). In development cohorts, oral-origin taxa that are hallmark CRC markers, such as *Fusobacterium nucleatum*, *Streptococcus oralis* and *Veillonella parvula*, explained most of the excess. Because the same taxa carry the cancer signal, cleaning the signature cannot remove the excess without losing cancer detection.
3. **A practical fix that uses data programmes already collect.** We propose a recalibration rule that uses only colonoscopy outcomes of test-positive people, with a finite-sample bound on the false-positive rate. In locked simulated deployments, it kept false alarms on target (0.6% of site-months above target vs 49% for the status quo), avoided about 83 unnecessary colonoscopies per 1,000 people screened at CRC sites, and kept sensitivity within 0.015 of an oracle cut-off. A per-group version removed the excess false alarms in heart failure and diabetes.

All methods, endpoints and hypotheses were frozen in a protocol before the locked cohorts were analysed. We report all results, including the hypotheses that were only partly supported. All data are public, and all code, the frozen protocol and the development log will be openly available.
